Concurrent Map: how the program works
======================================

Overview
--------
The program simulates a 48 by 30 map. Food is stored as an integer amount on
each tile. Cells move around the map, look for nearby food, and try to eat it
before their starvation deadline. A cell that eats ten times reproduces:
asexual cells create another asexual cell, while sexual cells seek a nearby
sexual partner and create one offspring together. A cell that fails to eat in
time dies and drops some food on its tile.

Each cell runs on its own Java thread. The simulation also has scheduled work
for adding food and writing statistics. Swing's event-dispatch thread refreshes
the map and statistics periodically. This means many threads can access shared
simulation data at once.

Colors in the map
-----------------
Green dots are food. A number on a green dot shows the amount on that tile when
the map was painted. Blue dots are sexual cells, and orange dots are asexual
cells. A hungry cell uses a darker shade of its type's color. Purple dots are
cells currently reproducing. If several cells occupy one tile, the drawing
offsets them slightly so they can be seen.

Where concurrency is controlled
--------------------------------
The population registry is a ConcurrentHashMap, so cells can be registered
and removed while other threads inspect it. Birth, death, and meal totals use
atomic counters. A cell's position and lifecycle state are volatile, so other
threads can see recent updates. Sexual pairing synchronizes on both cells in
ascending ID order; this prevents two suitors from claiming the same partner
and the consistent lock order avoids deadlock between those pair attempts.

These measures protect those specific operations. They do not make every map
observation a single, consistent snapshot: the UI can see values from different
moments while the simulation continues to run.

Intentional food race: food is not locked
-----------------------------------------
Food tiles and the shared total are plain integer fields in ResourceManager.
There is deliberately no lock around eating. tryConsumeAt checks whether a
tile has food, yields the thread, and then decrements the tile and total. If a
tile contains one unit, two cell threads can both read that it is positive
before either decrements it. Both then succeed in eating what was the same
last unit. The tile and total can become negative, and the meal count can
increase twice. This is the intended demonstration that an unlocked
check-then-act sequence is not atomic.

Food creation is also not protected. The scheduled food generator and dying
cells can add food concurrently with eaters and each other. The capacity check
and increments are separate operations too, so concurrent additions can lose
updates or pass the capacity limit. The displayed total and per-tile amounts
can disagree. The map renderer only draws a food dot when the copied tile
amount is positive, so a negative amount appears as no dot even though the
reported total may expose the race.

Other concurrency effects
-------------------------
The renderer copies food and cell collections while worker threads keep
changing them. Those copies are not atomic snapshots. The UI can briefly show
a mixture of old and new positions, cell states, or food amounts. For example,
a tile may be drawn before a cell moves there, or a food amount may change
while the array is being copied. These are transient visualization effects.

The registry, atomic counters, volatile cell fields, and synchronized mating
claim reduce races in their own areas. They do not protect food: leaving food
unlocked is intentional so multiple cells can race to eat the same unit.
